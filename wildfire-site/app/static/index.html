How it works

From 13 years of fires and weather to a daily watch list

01

Collect

18,303 fires from the US Forest Service fire-occurrence database, plus daily ERA5 weather for each area from 2012 to 2024.

02

Describe each day

For every area and day: recent heat, humidity, wind, rain over 1–30 days, dry streaks, and how unusual each is for that place and month.

03

Learn

A gradient-boosted tree model (120 trees, 27 inputs), trained on 2013–2021, blended 75/25 with each area's past fire rate.

04

Rank

Every evening, score all 24 areas for tomorrow and send crews to the top 3. Tested on 2022–2024, which the model never saw during training.

No peeking at the future

Every input for a target day uses data up to the day before. Training history stops at 31 Dec 2021 and the model is frozen after that, so the model never trained on any day shown on the map.

What the model leans on

How much the score quality drops when each input is scrambled (permutation importance, computed on LinuxONE when the server starts).

The accuracy trap

Only 12% of area-days have a fire. A "model" that always says no fire is 88% accurate and useless. Ours is 86% accurate at its alert setting, because it deliberately raises alerts to catch more fires. So we judge it on how well it ranks risky places, not on accuracy.

Honest limits

What this is, and isn't

A ranking, not a probability

Scores say which areas are riskier than others. They are not calibrated chances of fire.

Large areas

Each area is about 45 × 56 km with one weather point. It can't see terrain, fuels, power lines, lightning or people.

Hindsight weather

ERA5 is reanalysis made after the fact. Real forecasts are noisier, which is why the live version is the next step.

One region

Trained and tested on one part of Northern California. The test years were also looked at during development.
